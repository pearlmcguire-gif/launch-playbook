How I work

Four stories

Rolled up my sleeves

Seven malls, one date that couldn't move

MappedIn, an indoor mapping platform · implementation owner (contractor)
The situation
A major mall operator in Thailand was rolling out MappedIn across seven malls, timed to a big celebration at its largest mall. There was no room for extensions.
What made it hard
The client had very specific needs, many beyond what the product did at the time: a long list of feature requests, and late-night and early-morning calls.
What I did
I prioritized the two to three most urgent malls to go live first. I ran the project with the client's project manager and their 5-7 person team, while coordinating our Solutions, Data and Development teams. When Development needed details on the data-imports API that I couldn't give them, I brought the Data team into those calls directly.

How it worked: the flagship mall went live on time, and a mock preview reached the client company's President early.

Rolled up my sleeves

Answers around the clock

Eptura (formerly Proxyclick), a visitor management platform · Customer Success
The situation
Our support team covered Europe, Middle East and Africa (EMEA) and US hours. Clients in Asia-Pacific were mostly on their own during their workday.
What I did
I built Proxyclick's AI Help Center bot in Intercom from zero. I researched the questions clients asked most, then wrote the bot's content and macro templates that suggested articles automatically. It walked clients through step-by-step fixes, helped them troubleshoot, and sent them to the right article or video. As part of the same project I built a macro and prompt library for the support team.

How it worked: 40% of inquiries were resolved through AI chat and self-service, and the library cut second-tier escalations by 60%. Asia-Pacific clients had answers across almost all of the hours our team didn't cover. The project led to me owning all Help Center and academy content.

When a client didn't want to change

Moving a happy client to something new

MappedIn · self-serve platform launch (contractor)
The situation
MappedIn was launching a new self-serve platform, and our VP asked me to step in on the project. One client on my list ran a single-level outdoor outlet mall on our older Enterprise system. Not the ideal customer profile, but they liked the product.
Why they hesitated
Getting them on a call took a while. Once they joined, there were two reasons: they didn't want to take on the work of switching, and they were reviewing budgets, so any change in fees made them nervous.
What I did
I took the work off their plate. I had already built their map on the new platform, I set up their location myself, and since their contract was current, I gave them access so they could jump in and try it. I built a demo they could take back to their marketing and leadership teams. On budget, we were clear: they could keep their current setup, but they'd pay roughly two to three times more than on self-serve, and that cost would only keep growing and cause problems with their finance team. Self-serve would be much easier on their budget and on their teams. When they found missing features, I took the requests to Product.

How it worked: they chose the new self-serve map, even without the exact same feature set, because it covered what they needed. It also let them map beyond their property line, so they could add parking, driveways and directions from the highway as construction at the far end of their lot went up.

Teaching a client

The training gap behind a complaint

Pathmonk, a website lead-conversion platform · sole Customer Success hire (contractor)
The situation
A client mentioned in an unrelated email, about updating their content, that their lead results were dropping.
What I found
Discovery showed nobody had taught them how to read their dashboard or calculate their return on investment. It was a training gap, not a results problem.
What I did
Their content was in the middle of an update, so instead of anchoring to an outdated baseline, we set new benchmarks together. Then I followed up on a set schedule over the next two to three months.

How it worked: by the end, they could read and interpret the dashboard themselves.

And one I didn't let go of

At MappedIn I picked up an account flagged as challenging because its contacts weren't engaging. I dug into its history, found the real decision-maker, and within the first quarter had every thread going to the right people, contacts at more properties, account health up and two expansion conversations open.

How I'd run a Fulcrum launch

Six stages, and where owners hesitate

The middle four are Fulcrum's published Launch phases. Handoff, Refine and the handoff to Account Management are my proposal. Each card includes the moment I'd expect a shop owner to push back, and what I'd say. These are hypotheses from fulcrumpro.com and public reviews, and the first thing I'd check with your team.

Shaded: Fulcrum's Launch phases. Unshaded: my proposed stages.

A feedback and expansion log, from day one

Feature requests, custom tool ideas, modules held for later and anything Marketing should hear go into one log that travels with the shop. Product requests carry a status the shop can see. Not every request needs to land in the next sprint, but every request deserves a customer who knows where it stands.

A "what's new since kickoff" note at every stage

Fulcrum ships often, so the product at go-live can be ahead of the one the shop saw in the demo. A short note at each stage keeps the owner looking forward, and turns new releases into reasons to try something instead of surprises.

How I'd stay on top of it

Running a full launch load

With shops at every stage at once, the job is making sure none of them slips. Here's how I'd run my week. It's my plan, not a description of Fulcrum's process, and I'd shape it around how your team already works.

A weekly rhythm

Monday
Rank every shop on the board, block time for the top three, and confirm this week's deadlines with each shop.
Every day
Prep before each call. Log follow-ups and requests the same day. Reproduce setup issues before they go to Engineering.
Every week
A health check on every shop, with more check-ins during early rollout, when problems are most likely to show up.
Thursday
Update every launch summary and the feedback log, send Product the week's requests, and flag anything still open, so Friday is for wrapping it up.

Anything an owner will review is ready two days ahead, so there's time to fix a number at its source.

One place per shop

Each shop gets one home: the launch summary (goals, baselines, who's who, dates), the feedback and expansion log, and the "what's new since kickoff" note.

Anyone on the team can pick up a shop mid-launch without a briefing call, and the Account Manager inherits the full story.

What I'd automate first, with Claude

  • Call notes that draft an update to the launch summary, for me to review
  • Requests filed into the feedback log, tagged by type, shop and urgency
  • A draft "what's new since kickoff" note for each shop, built from release notes
  • Over time, simple queries that pull launch data straight into the board

Ideas to check with the team first. Each one hands time back to calls and building.
